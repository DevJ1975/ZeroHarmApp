Heat Stroke Is a 911 Call

Heat illness can go from a headache to a life-threatening emergency fast. The most important skill is knowing when to stop guessing and call 911.

Free this week: a 5-minute toolbox talk you can print, with a crew sign-in sheet.Download PDF

Talk it through with your crew

1. Spot the early signs. Headache, upset stomach, weakness, dizziness, heavy sweating, thirst. Stop work. Move the person somewhere cooler, give cool water, stay with them, and get them checked by medical.

2. Know the emergency signs. Confusion, acting strange, slurred speech, seizures or passing out. That is a medical emergency. Call 911.

3. Cool them right away. Do not wait for the ambulance. Soak them with cool water, put ice or cold packs on them, and fan them. Remove extra clothing and gear. Only give water if they are awake and can drink on their own.

4. Stay with them. Never leave the person alone. Send someone to meet the ambulance and lead them in. When in doubt, call 911.

CLOSE CALL · EXAMPLE STORY

He said he was fine

On a hot afternoon, a worker unloading trailers got quiet. Then he started fumbling his scanner and repeating himself. He said he was fine. His lead did not buy it. She called 911, and the crew cooled him with ice and wet towels until the ambulance came. The confusion was the warning sign.

WHAT OSHA SAYS

There is no federal OSHA heat standard yet. OSHA proposed one in 2024, and it is not final. [Check the rule’s status before sending.] OSHA still cites heat hazards under the General Duty Clause. Some states have their own heat rules, and they cover different kinds of workplaces. Check what applies in your state.

LAST WEEK’S ANSWER

A coworker is hanging in his harness and says he feels fine. How fast does he need to come down?

As fast as you can safely get him down. Feeling fine does not mean he is fine. Hanging in a harness can cut off blood flow in the legs, and a person can pass out without warning. Call 911 and start your rescue plan right away. Once he is down, get him checked by medical, even if he still says he is fine (1910.140(c)(21)).
